# Monetization and brand deals

Use this for income planning, pricing and pitching sponsorships, media kits, UGC work, affiliate and product strategy, and disclosure rules.

This is practical business guidance, not legal or tax advice. Rules differ by country; for contracts, tax and regulated industries, tell the user to check local rules or a qualified professional.

## 1. Income streams

| Stream | Works when | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Brand sponsorships | Engaged niche audience of almost any size | Income is lumpy; depends on fit and trust |
| UGC for brands | Good at making content; no audience needed | Paid for the content itself; brand posts it |
| Affiliate links | Audience trusts recommendations | Low effort; small unless volume or high-ticket |
| Platform payouts | Large view counts | Thresholds and rates vary and change; never the only plan |
| Own digital products | A clear problem the audience will pay to solve | Templates, guides, courses, presets |
| Services | Expertise the audience needs done for them | Coaching, consulting, freelance, agency |
| Memberships and subscriptions | A loyal core wanting more access | Needs steady delivery |
| Physical products and merchandise | Strong identity or a product niche | Stock, shipping and margins to manage |
| Events and workshops | Community with a shared interest | Online or in person |

Most stable creator businesses combine one owned offer (product or service) with one or two others, and build an email list to reduce dependence on any one platform.

## 2. Choosing what to sell

Work from the audience's problem:

1. List the questions and requests that come up most.
2. Find the one where a result is worth paying for.
3. Start with the smallest thing that delivers it (a paid workshop, a template, a one-to-one offer) and sell it before building anything large.
4. Use feedback and results as proof for the next version.

Fit the offer to audience size: small audiences support higher-priced services; large audiences support lower-priced products at volume.

## 3. Pricing sponsored content

There is no universal rate card. Build a price from components and state the reasoning.

**Base fee** for creating and posting the piece. Reference points creators use:

- **Cost per thousand views (CPM)**: fee = expected views / 1,000 x CPM. Use the creator's recent median views for that format, not their best post. The CPM depends heavily on niche, country and platform: specialist business, finance and technology audiences command several times the rate of broad entertainment.
- **Production value**: time to script, film, edit and revise, at a day rate the creator would accept.
- **Market check**: what similar creators in the same country and niche charge, when the user knows it.

Use the higher of the audience-based and the effort-based figure as the starting point.

**Add-ons**, each priced separately:

- Usage rights for the brand to run the content as ads (often 30 to 100 percent of the base fee per period, depending on length and scope)
- Whitelisting or partnership ads run through the creator's handle
- Exclusivity (compensation for turning down competitors for the period)
- Extra deliverables: stories, cut-downs, raw footage, extra hooks
- Rush delivery
- Link in bio, pinned placement, or keeping the post up for a guaranteed time

Give the user a worked example with their numbers and a three-tier package (basic, standard, premium) so the brand chooses between options instead of yes or no. Present ranges, and say plainly that market rates vary and that you cannot see current local rates without research.

For very small creators, a first few deals for product plus a modest fee can be reasonable to build a portfolio. Encourage moving to paid work quickly, and never working for "exposure" alone when the brand gets usage rights.

## 4. Usage rights, exclusivity and other terms

Points to check in any agreement:

- **Deliverables**: exact number, format, length, platforms, posting dates.
- **Approval**: how many revision rounds, and how fast the brand must respond.
- **Usage**: where the brand may use the content (organic, paid ads, website, print), for how long, in which countries. "In perpetuity" and "all media" should be narrowed or paid for.
- **Exclusivity**: which competitors, how long. Keep it narrow.
- **Payment**: amount, currency, schedule (ask for part upfront), due date, late terms, who covers fees and taxes.
- **Ownership**: creator normally keeps ownership and grants a licence.
- **Disclosure**: both sides agree it will be labelled as sponsored.
- **Claims**: the creator will only say what is true and what they experienced.
- **Cancellation and reshoots**: what happens if either side pulls out.
- **Performance clauses**: avoid guarantees of views or sales.

Get the terms in writing before making anything. Suggest a lawyer's review for large or long deals.

## 5. Media kit

One to three pages, clean and current. Sections:

1. **Who**: name, photo, one-line positioning, short bio.
2. **Audience**: size per platform, and who they are (age bands, locations, interests) from native analytics.
3. **Performance**: average views or reach per format, engagement rate, a recent standout result. Use recent real numbers and note the period.
4. **Content**: what the creator makes, with three to six examples.
5. **Past partners and results**: logos with permission, short case studies, testimonials.
6. **Services**: what is offered (dedicated video, integration, UGC, events, bundles).
7. **Rates**: optional; many creators send rates per enquiry.
8. **Contact**.

Never inflate numbers. Brands verify, and screenshots of native analytics are routinely requested.

## 6. Pitching brands

Target brands the creator already uses or that clearly fit the audience. Find the right contact (influencer, partnerships, social or brand marketing roles), then send a short pitch:

1. A specific, personal opening showing real familiarity with the brand.
2. Who the creator is and who the audience is, in one or two lines with a proof point.
3. One or two concrete content ideas for this brand.
4. What happens next: media kit attached, an offer to send rates or have a short call.

Keep it under 150 words. Follow up once after about a week, and once more later, then move on. A pitch template is in `templates.md`.

**Below a brand's follower minimum**: a threshold is usually a rough filter. Say the real number upfront, then make the case with recent average views, engagement rate and audience fit, backed by analytics screenshots. Offer alternatives that do not depend on audience size: a UGC video the brand posts itself, or a smaller first deal with an option to extend. Never close the gap with bought followers.

Warm the contact first where possible: tag the brand in organic content, engage with their posts, or come through a creator marketplace.

## 7. Negotiating

- Ask for the budget and the brief before quoting, when possible.
- Quote in packages; anchor on the standard tier.
- If the budget is lower, reduce scope (fewer deliverables, shorter usage) instead of cutting the price for the same work.
- Put a time limit on quotes.
- Stay polite and prompt; many deals come from repeat partners.
- Walk away from poor fits. One wrong sponsor costs more trust than it pays.

## 8. UGC creation for brands

UGC creators are paid to make content the brand publishes itself.

- **Portfolio**: a simple page with 6 to 10 sample videos across styles (testimonial, unboxing, demo, problem and solution, voiceover with b-roll), made with products already owned.
- **Pricing**: per video by length and complexity, with add-ons for extra hooks, raw footage, usage period and rush. Bundles of three or five videos are common.
- **Process**: brief, concept and script approval, filming, one or two revision rounds, delivery of files in the agreed ratio.
- **Scripts**: follow the short-video structures in `video-scripts-and-packaging.md`, with real, specific product experience.

A UGC testimonial must reflect the creator's honest experience of the product. Do not script claims the creator cannot stand behind, and do not present an actor's paid script as an unpaid customer review.

## 9. Delivering a sponsored piece

Sponsored content performs when it looks like the creator's best normal content:

- Build the integration around a real use, story or problem, in the creator's usual format.
- Hook on the audience's interest, not on the brand name.
- Show the product in use; include the one or two key messages the brand needs, in the creator's words.
- State the offer and the call to action clearly.
- Disclose at the start, using the platform's paid partnership tool.
- Afterwards, send the brand a short results summary with screenshots; it is the best pitch for a repeat deal.

Keep a sensible ratio of sponsored to regular content so the audience does not feel sold to.

## 10. Disclosure and legal basics

- **Disclose every material connection**: payment, free products, affiliate commission, discounts, family or business ties, ownership. This is a legal requirement in many countries (for example under the FTC in the United States, the ASA and CMA in the United Kingdom, and similar authorities across the EU, the Gulf and elsewhere), and a rule on every major platform.
- **Make it clear and early**: use the platform's paid partnership label, plus plain words such as "ad", "sponsored" or "paid partnership with ..." at the start of the caption or spoken in the video. Ambiguous tags (sp, collab, thanks) or disclosures buried after "see more" or among hashtags do not count. Affiliate links should be labelled as such.
- **Claims must be true and typical**: no invented results, no undisclosed editing of before-and-after images, extra care with health, weight, skin, finance and income claims. Some sectors need licences or specific wording. For supplements, food and wellness products, a creator can describe their own honest, modest experience ("I've felt less bloated after meals since I started"); they cannot say a product cures, treats or prevents a condition, promise a result in a set time, or imply the viewer will get the same outcome.
- **Rights**: use music the platform licenses for commercial or business use when content is sponsored; get permission for others' footage and images; get consent from people who appear, and take particular care with children.
- **Giveaways**: follow the platform's promotion rules and local law; state terms, eligibility, dates and how the winner is chosen.
- **Money**: sponsorship and gifted items are usually taxable income; keep records and invoices.

When a specific country's rule matters, say that it should be checked with the local regulator or a professional, and look it up when you have web access.

## 11. What to refuse

Advise against, and do not help produce:

- Sponsored content with the sponsorship hidden.
- Fake reviews, fake testimonials or invented results.
- Bought followers, views or engagement to reach a brand's threshold; inflated or edited analytics in a media kit.
- Promotion of products the creator has not tried when the script claims personal experience.
- Misleading health, financial or income promises.
- Deals with scam signals: paying a "fee" to become an ambassador, requests for passwords or account access, pressure to move fast without a contract, payments that require sending money back.

In each case explain the risk and give the honest alternative, such as a clearly labelled sponsored post that still reads naturally, or a media kit that leads with engagement quality and niche fit instead of raw follower count.
