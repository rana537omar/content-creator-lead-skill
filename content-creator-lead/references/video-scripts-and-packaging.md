# Video scripts and packaging

Use this for short video (Reels, TikTok, Shorts), long video (YouTube), podcasts, live sessions, and for titles and thumbnails.

## 1. Short video structure

Most effective short videos follow four beats:

| Beat | Time (for a 30 to 45 second video) | Job |
|---|---|---|
| Hook | 0 to 3 seconds | Stop the scroll, state or imply the promise |
| Setup | 3 to 8 seconds | Why this matters, the stake or the problem, in one or two lines |
| Payoff | The middle | The steps, story or demonstration. One idea, tightly cut |
| Close | Last 3 to 5 seconds | The final point or twist, plus one call to action |

Guidelines:

- **One idea per video.** A second idea is a second video.
- **Length follows content.** As short as the idea allows. A 20-second video watched to the end usually beats a 60-second one abandoned halfway. Go longer only when every second holds.
- **No slow open.** The first words are the hook. No greeting, no logo sting.
- **Deliver value before asking for anything.**
- **End cleanly.** Stop when the point lands. A close that loops back to the opening line encourages rewatches.

## 2. Short video script formats

Pick the format that fits the idea and the creator's comfort.

- **Talking head tip**: hook, problem, two or three steps, result, call to action.
- **List**: hook with the number, items in rising order of value, the best one last.
- **Story**: start at the moment of tension, back up briefly, what happened, what was learned.
- **Demonstration or tutorial**: show the finished result first, then the steps with on-screen labels.
- **Before and after**: the before, the change, the after, how to get it.
- **Myth and truth**: state the common belief, why it is wrong, what to do instead.
- **Reaction or teardown**: show the example, give the verdict, give the fix.
- **Day in the life or behind the scenes**: voiceover over b-roll with a thread that ties it together (a goal for the day, a problem to solve).
- **Reply to a comment**: show the comment, answer it directly.

Deliver a short script as a two-column table so it can be filmed directly:

| Time | Spoken (voice) | On screen (visual, text, b-roll) |
|---|---|---|
| 0-2s | "Your videos look cheap because of one setting." | Close-up, phone in hand. Text: "Fix this one setting" |
| 2-6s | ... | ... |

Add a line for the caption, a suggested cover text, and the call to action.

## 3. Writing for the ear

Scripts are spoken, so write the way people talk:

- Short sentences. One thought each.
- Contractions and everyday words. Read it aloud; rewrite anything that trips.
- Put the important word at the end of the sentence.
- Say "you" far more than "I".
- Use concrete numbers and examples in place of adjectives.
- About 140 to 160 spoken words per minute is a natural pace; short video often runs a little faster. Use that to estimate length.
- Mark pauses and emphasis where delivery matters.

For creators who dislike scripts, give bullet beats (hook line written out word for word, then prompts) so they can speak naturally.

## 4. Long video structure

For YouTube-style videos of roughly 8 to 20 minutes:

1. **Hook (first 15 to 30 seconds)**: confirm the title and thumbnail's promise, show the stake or the end result, and give a reason to stay ("by the end you will have...").
2. **Short context**: who this is for and why the creator can speak on it. Keep it brief; no long channel intro.
3. **Body in segments**: three to seven clear sections, each with its own mini hook, content and payoff. Order them for momentum: strong start, strongest near the end.
4. **Re-hooks between segments**: a line that opens the next gap ("that fixes the lighting, but it will not matter unless you get this next part right").
5. **Climax or key takeaway**: the most valuable part, placed late.
6. **Close**: a quick recap in a sentence or two, then one call to action that points to the next video to watch. End promptly; long goodbyes lose viewers.

Write the title and thumbnail concept **before** the script. If the package is weak, the script will not save it.

Deliver a long script with: title options, thumbnail concept, the hook written word for word, then each segment as headed talking points or full script (ask which the creator prefers), b-roll and graphic notes, and chapter timestamps to add after editing.

## 5. Retention techniques

- **Open loops**: promise something early and deliver it later. Close every loop you open.
- **Stakes**: say what goes wrong or what is gained.
- **Pattern changes**: change the shot, add b-roll, a graphic or a sound every few seconds in short video, every 20 to 40 seconds in long video. Change should serve the content, not distract from it.
- **Show, don't describe**: demonstrate on screen whenever possible.
- **Cut ruthlessly**: remove pauses, repeated points and tangents. If a section can go without the viewer noticing, it goes.
- **Captions on screen**: many people watch without sound.
- **Signposting**: "three steps", "the second mistake" helps viewers track progress.

When a retention graph is available: a steep drop in the first seconds means a hook or packaging mismatch; a drop at a specific point means that section drags or the promise was already paid; rises mark rewatched moments worth repeating.

## 6. Titles

A title's job is to earn the click from the right person while staying true to the video.

- Lead with the benefit or the curiosity; put key words early because titles are cut off on small screens.
- Keep it to roughly 40 to 60 characters where possible.
- Be specific: numbers, time frames, outcomes.
- For search-led topics, include the phrase people actually type.
- Title and thumbnail work as a pair: they should complete each other, not repeat the same words.
- Never promise what the video does not deliver.

Useful shapes: "How to [result] (without [pain])", "I tried [thing] for [time]", "[N] mistakes every [audience] makes", "Why [surprising statement]", "The [adjective] guide to [topic]", "[Thing] vs [thing]: which is [quality]?", "What I'd do if I started [topic] again".

Always give five to ten title options, name your pick and say why.

## 7. Thumbnails

Describe thumbnails as a brief a designer can execute:

- **One focal point**: a face with a clear expression, an object, or a before and after.
- **Three to five words of text at most**, large, adding something the title does not say. Often no text is better.
- **High contrast and simple background** so it reads at small size.
- **A curiosity element**: an arrow, a circle, a blurred result, a surprising pairing, used honestly.
- **Consistent style** across the channel so videos are recognisable.

Check it mentally at phone size and next to competing videos. Suggest two concepts when possible so the creator can test them.

Brief format: concept in one sentence, main image, expression or pose, text (if any), colours and background, what to avoid.

## 8. Podcasts and live sessions

**Podcast episode outline**: a cold open (the most interesting 20 to 30 seconds from the episode), a one-line promise, three to five segments with questions, a closing takeaway, the call to action. For interviews, prepare questions that draw stories and specifics ("tell me about the time...", "what did that cost you?") and avoid ones answered in the guest's bio. List the clips worth cutting for short video.

**Live session run-of-show**: title and promise, a timed outline (welcome and context, main teaching in blocks, audience questions, offer or next step), prompts to raise engagement, and a plan for the replay and clips.

## 9. Filming and editing notes to include

Where it helps the creator or editor, add short practical notes:

- Framing (vertical 9:16 for short video, horizontal 16:9 for long), eye-level camera, face a window or soft light, clean audio matters more than image quality.
- B-roll shot list: the specific shots that cover the voiceover.
- Text on screen: exact words and when they appear, kept inside the safe area away from interface buttons.
- Music and sound: mood and energy; use sounds the platform licenses for that account type.
- An edit note: pace, cuts, zooms, where to speed up.

Current length limits, aspect ratios and safe areas change; confirm them in the platform's help pages when precision matters.

## 10. Script review checklist

- Does the first line work with no context?
- Is the promise paid off, and is it worth the time asked?
- Could any sentence be cut with nothing lost?
- Does it sound like the creator when read aloud?
- Is there a visual for every stretch of voice?
- Is there exactly one call to action?
- Are all facts, numbers and quotes real?
