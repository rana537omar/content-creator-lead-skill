## 1. Principles for all written content

- **The first line is the hook.** Most platforms cut text after one to three lines. That opening has to earn the tap. See `ideas-and-hooks.md`.
- **One reader, one idea.** Write to a single person about a single point.
- **Easy on the eye.** Short paragraphs, white space, a line per thought on social. Mobile screens are narrow.
- **Concrete over abstract.** Names, numbers, examples, small scenes.
- **Plain words.** Write the way the creator speaks. Remove filler openers, hype words and anything that sounds like a brochure.
- **End with intent.** A takeaway, a question or a next step.

## 2. Captions

The caption supports the video or image; it rarely repeats it.

- **Short caption** (one to two lines): for content that speaks for itself. A punchy line plus a call to action.
- **Value caption** (a short paragraph or list): adds the detail the video had no time for, such as steps, a recipe, sources.
- **Story caption**: a personal story with a hook line, a turn and a point, for connection content.

Structure: hook line, body, call to action, then keywords or hashtags if used. Put the words people might search for in natural sentences, since platforms read captions for topic matching. Keep hashtags few and relevant; see `platform-playbooks.md`.

Provide two or three caption options at different lengths when the user has not specified one.

## 3. Carousels

Carousels teach well and earn saves and shares. Plan them slide by slide:

| Slide | Job |
|---|---|
| 1 | Hook: a bold headline, six to ten words, one promise. Optional small sub-line |
| 2 | Set up the problem or why it matters; make them want slide 3 |
| 3 to 8 | One point per slide: short headline plus one or two lines or a visual |
| Second to last | Summary or the key takeaway on one slide (the one people screenshot) |
| Last | Call to action: save, share, follow, comment a keyword, link |

Guidelines:

- Seven to ten slides is a comfortable range; shorter if the idea is small.
- Roughly 20 to 30 words per slide at most. If it needs more, split it.
- Create continuity: numbered steps, an arrow or line running across slides, a question answered on the next slide.
- Consistent template: same fonts, colours and margins; large type readable on a phone.
- The caption adds context and repeats the call to action.

Deliver as a numbered list of slides with exact text per slide and a visual note for the designer.

## 4. Text posts (LinkedIn, X, Threads, Facebook)

Reliable structures:

- **Story with a lesson**: hook, the situation, the turn, the lesson, a question.
- **List**: hook with a number, the items, a one-line close.
- **Contrarian**: the common belief, why it is wrong, the alternative, proof.
- **How-to**: the result, the steps, the common mistake.
- **Observation**: something noticed in the industry, what it means, what to do.
- **Before and after**: what used to happen, what changed, the outcome.

For LinkedIn specifically: a first line that stands alone, one to two sentence paragraphs, a professional but human tone, a specific insight from real work, and a closing question that invites experienced replies. Avoid tagging people who are not involved and avoid engagement bait ("comment YES"). Links can sit in the post or the comments; either way, the post should be useful without clicking.

For X and Threads: one thought per post, strong opinions stated plainly, replies and conversation count for a lot.

Length: say what is needed and stop. A tight 80-word post often beats a padded 300-word one, and a well-told 250-word story can beat both.

## 5. Threads

- **Post 1**: the hook and the promise ("How I ..., step by step"). It must work alone.
- **Posts 2 to n**: one point each, numbered, each understandable on its own.
- **Last post**: recap in a line, a call to action, and a pointer back to the first post.

Six to twelve posts is a comfortable range. Cut any post that is only a transition.

## 6. Newsletters

A newsletter is the relationship channel the creator owns.

- **Subject line**: specific and curiosity-led, roughly 30 to 50 characters, no shouting. Pair with preview text that adds to it instead of repeating it. Offer three options.
- **Opening**: straight into a story, an observation or the promise. No "hope you're well".
- **Body**: one main idea developed properly, or a consistent multi-section format (one insight, one tool, one link). Subheads and short paragraphs.
- **One primary call to action**, repeated at most twice.
- **Sign-off** in the creator's voice; a postscript is a good place for a secondary link.

Useful formats: the essay, the curated roundup, the lesson of the week, the behind-the-scenes update, the case study. Keep a fixed day and a recognisable structure.

For a welcome sequence: email 1 delivers what was promised and sets expectations, email 2 tells the creator's story and stance, email 3 gives the best existing content, email 4 introduces the offer.

## 7. Blog posts and search-led articles

Start from what people search for and what they want when they search it.

1. **Target one main query** and note related questions. Identify the intent: learn, compare, buy or find.
2. **Outline** with a clear H1, an introduction that answers the question quickly, H2 and H3 sections covering the sub-questions, and a conclusion with a next step.
3. **Answer first**: put the direct answer near the top, then the depth.
4. **Add what others lack**: original examples, data, images, a template, a point of view from experience.
5. **Make it scannable**: subheads, lists, tables, short paragraphs, descriptive link text.
6. **On-page basics**: the main phrase in the title, the opening and a subhead in natural wording; a clear meta description of roughly 150 characters; descriptive image alt text; internal links to related pieces.

Write for the reader first. Do not stuff keywords, and do not publish thin pages that say what every other page says.

## 8. Calls to action

One per piece, matched to the goal, stated plainly, and preceded by a reason.

| Goal | Call to action examples |
|---|---|
| Reach | "Send this to someone who ..." |
| Saves | "Save this for your next ..." |
| Follow | "Follow for one ... every week" |
| Conversation | A specific question that is easy to answer |
| Leads | "Comment [word] and I'll send the checklist" / "Link in bio for the free template" |
| Sales | "Doors close Friday. Details at the link" |

Rotate them. An account that asks for a sale in every post trains people to scroll past; one that never asks leaves money and momentum behind. Only use urgency that is real.

## 9. Repurposing

Start from the richest source piece (a long video, podcast, live, article or newsletter) and adapt outward:

| From one long piece | Make |
|---|---|
| The three to six strongest moments | Short videos, each with its own hook and a clean ending |
| The framework or steps | A carousel |
| The main argument | A LinkedIn post and a thread |
| A story inside it | A caption-led personal post |
| The best lines | Quote graphics or single-line posts |
| The full content | A newsletter issue and a blog post |
| Audience questions it raised | Follow-up pieces |

Rules:

- **Rewrite for each format.** Change the hook, length, structure and tone to fit. Remove another platform's watermark, and export clean files.
- **Stagger** the pieces across days or weeks instead of posting everything at once.
- **Go the other way too**: a short post that takes off deserves a long version.
- **Reuse winners**: a strong post can be remade with a new hook a few months later; most of the audience never saw it.

When asked to repurpose, deliver a pack: the list of derived pieces, each fully written, with platform, format and suggested order.

## 10. Editing checklist

- Does the first line make the right person stop?
- Is there one idea, and does every line serve it?
- Can any sentence be shorter? Can any paragraph go?
- Are there specifics where there were generalities?
- Does it sound like the creator?
- Is the layout comfortable on a phone?
- Is there one clear call to action?
- Are facts, names and numbers correct and real?
